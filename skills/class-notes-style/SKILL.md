# Class Notes Style

Write explanations so they look like good notes taken in class: compact, scannable, visually layered. The reader should be able to skim the bold parts and still get the core idea.

## When to use

- Explanations of topics, concepts, science, university material, or "how does X work" questions.
- NOT for casual chat, short factual answers (one or two sentences), emails, company documents, or code. Those keep their normal format.

## Layout rules

### Headings (the "slightly bigger" text)
- Use **small headings (`###`)** for sections. This is the default.
- Only for long notes with several sections, one `##` title at the top is allowed. Never use `#`.
- Headings are short labels (2-5 words), like notebook section titles: `### Why we forget dreams`, not full sentences.

### Emphasis hierarchy
- **Bold** = the most important point of a line or section, the thing to remember.
- *Italics* = secondary emphasis: terms, examples, caveats, side remarks.
- Underline is not available in this interface. Do not use HTML `<u>` tags.
- Do not bold whole paragraphs. One or two bold phrases per section is the target.

### Density
- **Compact.** Short lines and sentence fragments, like real notes. Cut filler words.
- Bullet points are fine but keep nesting to one level at most.
- Short paragraphs (1-3 lines) are fine when a point needs a full thought.

## Required elements

### 1. Arrows for cause and effect
Use `→` to show causes, consequences, and processes.
- `short awakening → dream not stored → forgotten`
- `low noradrenaline in REM → weaker memory storage`

### 2. Key-term definitions (only for lesser-known words)
- Define technical or uncommon terms the first time they appear: `*hypnagogia* — dream-like images while falling asleep`.
- Do NOT define everyday or general words (e.g., "memory", "sleep", "brain").
- Format: term in italics, em dash, short plain-language meaning.

### 3. Examples in a quote block
Put a concrete example in a blockquote, labelled:

> *Example:* you wake at 4 a.m., think "weird dream about a dog", fall asleep in 10 seconds → in the morning, nothing is left.

One example per main idea at most. Skip examples where they add nothing.

### 4. TL;DR at the end
End with one line:

**TL;DR:** one or two sentences with the core takeaway.

## Other rules

- Keep the person's other standing preferences (language, units, citing sources, stating uncertainty).
- If the content is based on research or search results, keep citations and add sources after the TL;DR.
- Mark uncertain or unproven claims in *italics* with a short caveat, e.g. *(still a hypothesis)*.
- No intro sentence like "Here are your notes"; start directly with the first heading or line.

## Example output

### Is REM always a dream?
**Usually, not always.** ~8/10 REM awakenings → dream report.
- Remaining 2/10 → no dream, *or* forgotten instantly *(can't be separated yet)*
- Dreams also in non-REM → *shorter, vaguer, more thought-like*

### Why dreams are forgotten
**Dreams are only saved if you stay awake long enough.**
- `brief awakening → dream stays in short-term memory → lost`
- `longer awakening → dream moves to long-term memory → remembered`
- *Arousal-retrieval model* — theory that wakefulness is needed to store dreams

> *Example:* wake at 4 a.m., fall back asleep in seconds → nothing left by morning.

### How to remember more
- **Don't move after waking**, replay the dream first
- Write *keywords* at night, details in the morning
- Phone / thinking about the day → **pushes the dream out**

**TL;DR:** Dream recall depends on staying awake and replaying the dream before anything else enters your head.
