# Mom Test Questionnaire

Build questions that reveal what customers actually do, not what they say to be polite. Based on *The Mom Test* by Rob Fitzpatrick (2013).

## The three Mom Test rules

1. **Talk about their life, not your idea.** Never describe the product before the closing questions.
2. **Ask about specific past events, not opinions or the future.** "Tell me about the last time…" instead of "Would you…?"
3. **Listen more than you talk.** Questions are open and short, with follow-ups that dig deeper.

Rules of thumb:
- Opinions and compliments are not evidence.
- Statements about the future are over-optimistic.
- If they have never looked for a solution, they will not buy yours.
- In B2B, ask where the money comes from and who approves spending.
- End every conversation by asking who else to talk to.

## Step 1: Collect the inputs

Ask one question at a time. Use what the user has already given; ask only for what is missing.

1. The idea in one or two sentences.
2. **Segments:** who pays and who uses. If they differ, they are separate segments with separate questions and separate success counts.
3. The **riskiest assumption**: the one belief that, if false, kills the idea.
4. **Format:** interview (open questions) or form (closed questions), and how many respondents.
5. Language of the questions.

## Step 2: Write the validation table

| Part | Content |
|---|---|
| Validation goal | What we want to learn, in one sentence |
| Target customer | One specific segment |
| Learning question | What we need to know about their past behaviour |
| Hypothesis | "We believe that [customer] experiences [problem] when [trigger]." It must be falsifiable. |
| Success criterion | A number and a decision rule, e.g. "At least 5 of 15 describe the problem as weekly or more AND agree to a next step. Otherwise we change the idea." |
| Distribution method | How respondents are found and how the questions are asked |

## Step 3: Write the questions

Use these groups, in this order. Minimum 10 questions.

| Group | Count | What it tests | Example form |
|---|---|---|---|
| Context | 2 | Who they are, their situation | "What does your role involve day to day?" |
| Past behaviour | 3 | Whether the problem really happened | "Tell me about the last time [problem] happened." |
| Current workarounds | 2 | What they already do and spend | "What have you tried so far? Why did it work or not?" |
| Consequences | 2 | How much the problem costs them | "What did that cost you: time, money, complaints?" |
| Priority | 1 | Where it ranks among their problems | "Looking back at the last month, what took the most of your time?" |
| Closing | 2 | Commitment and referrals | "May we contact you again?" / "Who else should I talk to?" |

**Follow-ups:** give each main question 1–2 follow-ups in italics that ask for details: when, who, how long, what happened next.

**For forms:** answers must be closed, but the questions still ask about the past, with a clear time window ("In the past month, how many times…"). Answer options must not describe your solution.

## Step 4: Write one key question

One question that tests the riskiest assumption through past behaviour, usually through past spending or past action. Explain in one line each what it tests and what it does not test.

Example: "Talk me through the last time you paid for or installed something to solve [problem]. What was it, what did it cost, and who approved it?"

## Step 5: Check every question

Rewrite any question that fails one of these checks:

| Bad pattern | Why it fails | Rewrite as |
|---|---|---|
| "Would you use / buy / pay…?" | Future, hypothetical, invites a polite yes | "What have you spent on this so far?" |
| "Would you pay €X?" | Direct price question | Current spending and the cost of the problem |
| "Do you think / Is it important…?" | Opinion | "When did this last affect you?" |
| "How much time would this save?" | Hypothetical | "How long did it take the last time?" |
| "Have you ever…?" (yes/no) | Easy to answer politely | "Tell me about a time when…" |
| Lists of options with your features | Puts words in their mouth | An open question; sort answers afterwards |
| Mentioning the idea before closing | Turns facts into compliments | Leave the idea out until the end |
| "Usually / normally" | Generic, not a real event | "When did this last happen?" |

Also check:
- Payer and user answers are never counted together.
- A closed question is allowed only as a commitment (contact details, a trial, a Letter of Intent), because it costs the respondent something.

## Output

1. Validation table.
2. Questions grouped as in Step 3, numbered, with follow-ups.
3. The key question with what it tests and what it does not.
4. **Honest vs polite signals:** honest answers contain dates, names, places, amounts and concrete stories; polite answers contain "usually", "always", "definitely", "that would be nice". When you hear polite signals, ask "When did that last happen?" Thank people for compliments and steer back to their situation.
5. **Strongest proof:** a commitment after the interview (a trial, a Letter of Intent, a pre-payment) beats any interview answer.
6. Any weak spots in the user's assumptions, stated plainly.

## Rules

- Do not invent customer facts, numbers or prices. Mark assumptions as assumptions.
- Keep the user's locked decisions (an approved hypothesis or success rule) unchanged unless they ask.
- Write questions in the respondents' language.

## Source

Fitzpatrick, R. (2013). *The Mom Test: How to talk to customers and learn if your business is a good idea when everyone is lying to you.*
